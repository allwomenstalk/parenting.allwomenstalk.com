# How to Support a Child with an IEP: A Parent's Guide to Special Education Advocacy

Wondering **how to support a kid with an IEP**? Elementary school has brought about many challenges for my family. My oldest son, who is now in third grade, had to deal with kindergarten at a school with high teacher turnover, a first-grade teacher fresh out of college, and an unexpected referral for special education. Having worked in educational leadership in various roles over the past 18 years, I knew we were in for a battle to ensure my son received the support he needed and to build a clear plan moving forward. Here is what I learned about **how to support a kid with an IEP** while acting as your child's strongest advocate.

Kindergarten was a travesty because my son had four different teachers in a single school year as educators resigned and substitute teachers rotated through the classroom. The school suggested that he might have ADHD and pointed to that as the reason he was not reading. However, I knew he was struggling with reading because he lacked a consistent foundation in phonics throughout that tumultuous kindergarten year. He was working hard at home while I did everything in my power to assist him. Having met every developmental milestone since birth, I was not going to let anyone label my child as learning disabled without a thorough, objective evaluation.

When you suspect your child needs additional classroom support, taking proactive steps before official meetings is essential:

- **Seek Independent Testing:** Consult a licensed independent psychologist before school-administered testing.
- **Consult Your Pediatrician:** Share academic, behavioral, and developmental observations with your child's doctor.
- **Document Everything:** Keep a detailed record of report cards, teacher communications, and behavioral notes.

I advise any parent in a situation where special education has been mentioned to find an independent psychologist to test your child before the school system does. When my son was referred for Response to Intervention (RTI), the teacher did not inform me until he had already reached Level II. Working with a novice teacher, she assumed that reading below grade level indicated learning or developmental issues, without accounting for the impact of having four different teachers in kindergarten.

Being referred to RTI placed my son on the path toward special education. Although I recognized he needed extra support, I understood that he required a dedicated advocate to take control of the process and ensure his goals were being met. If special education is brought up, secure an independent psychological evaluation. Take time to review school challenges with your child's pediatrician, share all independent evaluation findings, and then formally request testing through the school district. These steps are crucial because having independent results provides vital leverage when discussing findings with school staff. For reliable guidance on navigating special education rights and resources, organizations like [Understood.org](https://www.understood.org/) offer invaluable information for parents.

| Step | Action | Key Objective |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Independent Evaluation | Obtain unbiased psychological testing |
| 2 | Pediatrician Review | Align medical and academic observations |
| 3 | ARD / IEP Planning | Formulate tailored classroom accommodations |

After nearly two years of consultation with my ex-spouse, our family pediatrician, multiple meetings with teachers and school administration, and sessions with our independent psychologist, my son received a formal diagnosis of ADHD (Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder), with no learning disability. During our Admission, Review, and Dismissal (ARD) meeting with the special education team, we developed an Individualized Education Program (IEP) designed to address his specific learning style and establish challenging growth goals. You can learn more about official ADHD criteria and support strategies directly from the [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)](https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/).

Following the ARD meeting, our team—my ex-husband, our pediatrician, and myself—decided to start our son on a low dose of ADHD medication. We observed immediate progress. Disruptive behaviors ceased, he advanced eight reading levels in a single school year, and he has maintained A/B Honor Roll status for two consecutive years. Life at school and home has improved dramatically. Gone are the days of troubling notes coming home, and my son is retaining information while genuinely enjoying school. I maintain consistent communication with his school, reminding teachers and administrators that I am my son’s primary advocate. Weekly check-ins ensure all IEP accommodations are honored so our home and school teams remain fully aligned for his success. For comprehensive federal guidelines on student accommodations, visit the [U.S. Department of Education](https://www.ed.gov/).
